# Event Horizon Fund Deep Dive Comparison and Recommendations (Jan 2026)

## Overview of Analyses

Two in-depth reviews – one by **GPT-5.2** and one by **Gemini** – were conducted on the Event Horizon Fund, a portfolio focused on AI infrastructure. Both analyses validate the fund’s core thesis and performance through 2025, while offering nuanced recommendations for 2026. Below, we compare their key points of **agreement** and **difference**, then assess how the fund aligns with current AI/datacenter trends. Finally, we evaluate whether the **current allocation** remains optimal and suggest any portfolio changes (with the “Moonshot” sleeve as an optional component).

## Agreements Between GPT-5.2 and Gemini Analyses

Both deep dives come to very similar conclusions about the Event Horizon Fund’s strategy and holdings:

- **Thesis Validation: GPT-5.2 and Gemini agree that the fund’s focus on AI’s physical bottlenecks has been powerfully validated by real-world trends in 2025.** The fund’s premise – that value would accrue to choke-point providers (chip fabs, fab equipment, power, cooling, etc.) rather than just hyped AI end-products – **“has not only been validated but accelerated”** by industry developments. Each analysis notes that AI investment has shifted into an **“industrial hardening” phase** where constraints are physical (compute, energy, supply chain) rather than algorithmic. This confirms the portfolio’s concentration in **“hard infrastructure”** plays was well-chosen. Both reports highlight that 2025’s AI frenzy transitioned into a **massive capex cycle** by major tech firms, creating supply-demand imbalances that benefited the fund’s holdings.

- **Core Portfolio Emphasis:** Both analyses emphasize **holding the fund’s core positions at their current weightings**, given their critical role as bottlenecks:

- **TSMC (~12%) – No Substitute Gatekeeper:** Both call Taiwan Semi the *“singular/indispensable chokepoint”* for advanced AI chips and concur it should remain a top allocation. TSMC’s near-monopoly on \<5 nm fabrication and advanced packaging (CoWoS) gives it durable pricing power as AI demand outstrips supply. Both analyses note TSMC can **sell every wafer it produces** at premium prices for years to come, due to structural undercapacity. In short, they **strongly agree on keeping TSMC at ~12%** as a cornerstone holding.

- **Nvidia (~6%) + Broadcom (~3%) – Balanced AI Chip Exposure:** Both analyses support the combined ~9% weight in **AI accelerators**, split between Nvidia and Broadcom. They agree Nvidia remains the *“undisputed leader”* in AI training with ~80–90% market share, and thus some exposure is essential despite its high valuation. Meanwhile, Broadcom’s custom ASIC business (e.g. Google TPUs) is a key **hedge against Nvidia’s dominance**, ensuring the portfolio also benefits from the hyperscalers’ move to in-house chips. **GPT-5.2** deliberately kept this sleeve at \<10% to avoid over-concentration, and **Gemini** concurs: it recommends **maintaining the 6% NVDA / 3% AVGO split** to capture both the training GPU boom and the ASIC trend.

- **Micron (6%) – Memory Oligopoly:** Both note that High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) has become a *scarce, strategic resource* for AI. **GPT-5.2** and **Gemini** each highlight that only three companies make HBM and that **Micron’s HBM output is effectively sold out through 2026** – a dramatic shift from memory being a commodity to being a bottleneck. They agree Micron’s 6% weight is appropriate: it has huge upside from AI demand, yet memory can be cyclical, so 6% is a moderate allocation. Both recommend **holding Micron at 6%** to ride this HBM super-cycle.

- **ASML (8%) + Applied Materials (4%) – Fab Equipment:** Both analyses strongly endorse the **12% combined in semiconductor equipment** (ASML’s EUV lithography monopoly and AMAT’s broader fab tool coverage). The reasoning: every advanced chip (made by TSMC, Intel, etc.) **requires ASML’s EUV machines**, and ASML cannot be easily bypassed. **GPT-5.2** calls ASML *“hard to imagine the AI 2027 thesis without”* and would buy more on dips. **Gemini** likewise keeps ASML at 8% as a core bottleneck play. Applied Materials at 4% is also maintained, as chipmakers’ multi-year fab expansion plans (TSMC in Arizona, Intel in Ohio/Germany, Samsung in Texas, etc.) mean high tool demand. Both analyses say **hold WFE at ~12% total**.

- **Synopsys (5%) – EDA Software:** There’s agreement that owning an **electronic design automation (EDA)** leader is important for upstream exposure. **Synopsys at ~5%** is kept in both cases. The rationale: **“no advanced chips can be built without EDA tools,”** and Synopsys (plus Cadence) form a duopoly tollbooth on all new chip designs. Both consider 5% in SNPS a prudent, diversified way to benefit from the explosion of chip R&D across the industry.

- **Arista Networks (5%) – AI Networking:** Both deep dives highlight that **networking bandwidth** inside AI data centers has become a critical constraint. They note the industry’s shift toward ultra-fast Ethernet switching (400G/800G) to connect GPU clusters, which **puts Arista at the center of AI networking upgrades**. The fund’s **5% allocation to Arista** is affirmed by both; **GPT-5.2** rates it a hold (with potential to increase if Ethernet fully wins out), and **Gemini** likewise lists ANET at 5% (Strong Hold) in its target mix. *Both agree Arista is the best pure-play on the data center “fabric” transition for AI.*

- **Vertiv (8%) + Dell (5%) – Power/Cooling & Systems:** There is consensus that **Vertiv’s 8% and Dell’s 5% weights** (13% combined for “rack-scale integration & cooling”) remain warranted. **GPT-5.2** notes Vertiv aligns perfectly with the fund’s bottleneck theme (cooling is mandatory for high-density AI racks) and even suggests considering **increasing Vertiv’s allocation** beyond 8% given its order surge. **Gemini** also documents Vertiv’s dominance in liquid cooling with record backlogs, keeping it at 8% (Strong Hold). **Dell** is seen as the **execution leader in deploying AI servers at scale**, complementing Vertiv. Both analyses credit Dell’s inclusion (5%) as a wise move – it’s capturing the massive “integrated AI rack” market, while being a stable, cash-generative company. **GPT-5.2** validates Dell’s 5% weight (providing meaningful exposure without overdoing it), and **Gemini** explicitly recommends swapping out any Super Micro in favor of Dell (more on that under differences). In short, **both agree on ~13% allocated to the data center hardware layer (Vertiv+Dell)**.

- **Constellation Energy (15%) + Quanta Services (3%) – Energy Infrastructure:** Both analyses underscore that **power has emerged as perhaps the most crucial AI bottleneck**, and they maintain a heavy 18% combined allocation here. **Constellation (CEG) at ~15%** is identified by both as a *highest-conviction* holding. The GPT-5.2 report calls CEG **“one of the highest-conviction positions”**, noting Big Tech’s multi-decade nuclear power deals have *“re-rated”* nuclear from a commodity to a scarce enabler. Gemini concurs, citing CEG’s long-term contracts (e.g. Meta’s 20-year PPA) and **premium PPA pricing (\$85–\$115/MWh)** that dramatically boost margins. Both also approve of **Quanta Services (3%)** as a niche position addressing grid bottlenecks. Quanta builds transmission lines and substations – an urgent need when **50 GW of new AI-driven demand is hitting the grid by 2027**. **GPT-5.2** added Quanta as a small position later, with the logic that it **“fills the gap”** on power delivery and should be kept (with potential to add on dips). **Gemini** calls Quanta a Buy at 3%, emphasizing that **grid lead-times are so long** that Quanta’s services have inelastic demand. *In sum, both analyses strongly support the fund’s large 18% allocation to power generation (CEG) and grid buildout (PWR).*

- **Small Satellite Positions (2–3% each):** Both analyses agree on retaining **small, speculative positions at the ~1–3% level** in several thematic areas:
  - **AI-Native Security – CrowdStrike (2%):** Both reports favor CrowdStrike as the pick for AI-era cybersecurity. **GPT-5.2** notes CrowdStrike is a *“must-have”* in securing AI workloads, and suggests a slight increase (toward 3%) over time[\[1\]](file://file_00000000532c71fdb514e9f4f39df014#:~:text=Recommendation%3A%20Consider%20a%20slight%20increase,CrowdStrike%20is%20arguably%20the%20%E2%80%9Cmust). **Gemini** similarly tags CRWD 2% as *“Buy”* – it dropped legacy Palo Alto Networks in favor of CrowdStrike’s AI-focused approach. Both clearly view CRWD’s small allocation as worthwhile and potentially growing as AI-driven cyber threats rise.
  - **AI-Native Operations – Palantir (2%):** Both analyses keep **Palantir at 2%** as a play on AI-driven decision-making software for enterprises/government. **GPT-5.2** sees 2% as enough to benefit if Palantir’s AI platform gains traction, without too much risk. **Gemini** calls Palantir the *“operating system for the AI enterprise”* and shows it’s rapidly converting customers (121% YoY U.S. commercial growth in late 2025). Both are cautiously optimistic – hold a small stake to capture upside from Palantir’s unique positioning, but **don’t overweight it** until its AI business further proves out.
  - **Defense/Autonomy – AeroVironment & Kratos (1% each):** Both agree on keeping **tiny positions (∼1% each)** in these defense tech names as a hedge on AI’s militarization. **GPT-5.2** frames AVAV and KTOS as *“toe-hold”* positions that could **“multi-bag”** if an AI arms race accelerates, while limiting risk via small size. **Gemini** also includes these names (combined ~2–3%) noting recent drone program wins for each (e.g. Kratos’s Valkyrie and AVAV’s Switchblade getting DoD contracts). Both analyses acknowledge these are higher-risk, but agree the asymmetric payoff justifies a modest inclusion.
  - **Bio-Risk Hedge – Moderna (1%):** Both analyses support **Moderna at ~1%** as a tail-risk hedge against biothreats (which could be exacerbated by AI). **GPT-5.2** calls it a *“cheap insurance policy”* – you don’t expect it to pay off unless an engineered pandemic occurs. **Gemini** likewise keeps 1% in MRNA, citing its rapid mRNA platform as the fastest responder if an AI-enabled bio-event happened. In essence, **both keep Moderna as a small hedge and see no strong reason to change it** now that COVID is waning; it remains *“just in case.”*
  - **Strategic Materials – MP Materials (3%):** Both analyses agree on **MP at ~3%** as a geopolitical hedge (rare earth supply). **GPT-5.2** argues 3% is appropriate – if rare earths become “the new oil” in a U.S.–China trade war, MP could surge, and at 3% it would contribute meaningfully. If nothing major happens, a 3% mining stock won’t drag much. **Gemini** similarly notes MP Materials has now *“graduated to a strategic national asset”* in the eyes of the U.S. (thanks to the **CHIPS Act and defense initiatives**). It specifically mentions MP’s new domestic magnet factory coming online as a unique advantage. Thus, **both favor keeping MP at ~3%** for its option value in a supply-chain squeeze scenario.

- **Avoiding Hype & Overlap:** Importantly, both analyses reiterate the **fund’s conscious *exclusion* of the most hyped AI plays (and other overlapping names)**:

- Neither recommends adding the big cloud hyperscalers (Amazon, Google, Microsoft, etc.) despite their AI investments. In fact, the **Gemini report explicitly concludes that excluding hyperscalers remains sound**, because those giants are *buyers* of scarce resources (GPUs, power) rather than providers – **they’re on the “wrong side” of the supply/demand imbalance** the fund exploits. **GPT-5.2** made a similar point: owning “the picks and shovels” is better than being the *marginal buyer* of GPUs or AI services.

- Both also caution against adding seemingly related chip names that **don’t own a bottleneck**. For example, **GPT-5.2** considered AMD (a rising \#2 in AI GPUs) and decided **not to add it**, since the portfolio already captures AMD’s success indirectly (through TSMC manufacturing its chips, and Micron supplying HBM) and AMD doesn’t control any unique supply choke-point. This logic aligns with the fund’s focus on **monopoly-like positions**. **Gemini** did not list AMD anywhere either, implying agreement that **Nvidia + TSMC (and Broadcom as a hedge)** sufficiently cover AI processors without introducing AMD’s execution risk.

- Both analyses stick to the **initial roster of sectors/stocks**, not introducing entirely new categories. They did *consider* some (GPT-5.2 mentioned power semiconductors like SiC/GaN as a question, but concluded those aren’t a clear bottleneck yet given multiple suppliers)[\[2\]](file://file_00000000532c71fdb514e9f4f39df014#:~:text=Power%20Semiconductors%20,Wolfspeed%2C%20ON%20Semi). Thus, **both analyses affirm the fund’s existing scope** – which spans semiconductors, infrastructure, and select software – is comprehensive for the AI thesis, with no obvious gaps.

In summary, **GPT-5.2 and Gemini are overwhelmingly in agreement** on the Event Horizon Fund’s composition. Both found that 2025’s events **“delivered substantial alpha”** for the fund’s holdings and that the portfolio is aligned with the **“operational reality”** of early 2026’s AI landscape. They independently validate each sector’s inclusion and generally endorse the **same allocation weights**, with only minor tactical nuances (discussed next).

## Key Differences Between GPT-5.2 and Gemini Analyses

While the two deep dives agree on fundamentals, a few recommendations diverge due to differences in emphasis or updated information:

- **♢ Intel’s Role – Moonshot vs. Core:** The **biggest divergence is on Intel (INTC)**. The GPT-5.2 report treats Intel as a **speculative “moonshot”** holding (in the optional 10% sleeve) rather than a core position. At the time of that analysis, Intel’s foundry turnaround was still unproven – **GPT-5.2 noted Intel trailed TSMC by years**, advising to **keep INTC as a high-risk LEAPS position** rather than part of the main portfolio. It explicitly states *“no core position in INTC yet, consistent with ‘keep Intel as a moonshot, not core’”*.

**Gemini, however, argues that by Jan 2026 Intel has “graduated” from moonshot to core**. It highlights **new developments**: Intel’s 18A process node achieved high-volume manufacturing with solid yields, and Intel won a foundry contract with Apple. These are game-changers that *“de-risk the thesis”*. As a result, the Gemini report recommends **promoting Intel to the core portfolio with a 5–6% allocation**. This essentially moves Intel from the optional LEAPS sleeve into the main fund. Gemini also notes the U.S. government’s heavy support (CHIPS Act funding giving it a partial stake in Intel) as a “sovereign floor” under Intel’s stock.

*In short:* GPT-5.2 was cautious on Intel (high risk, keep it small/derivative), whereas **Gemini is bullish due to Intel’s validated progress**, urging the fund to **buy and hold Intel equity outright** going forward. This difference stems from timing – the GPT analysis might pre-date or de-emphasize the concrete proof of Intel’s turnaround, while Gemini incorporates the latest Jan 2026 news that **Intel “leapfrogged” certain nodes and secured a whale customer**[\[3\]](file://file_0000000074d471f8be0caa34eba20910#:~:text=match%20at%20L166%20%28AAPL%29,deficit%20that%20plagued%20previous%20efforts). This is a significant strategic shift: the fund originally avoided Intel except via options, but now it may warrant a meaningful core stake as a **domestic hedge and high-upside play**. (We will reconcile this in the recommendations section.)

- **♢ Simplifying the Moonshot Sleeve:** Another difference is **how to handle the optional 10% Moonshot sleeve (risky, asymmetric bets)**. **GPT-5.2’s** report largely leaves the Moonshot sleeve as-is (unfunded but proposed), implying the use of LEAPS/options on names like Intel, Kratos, etc., for high leverage if one chooses to deploy it. It doesn’t elaborate much, effectively treating it as **unchanged and up to the investor** (it even refers to *“our plan to use options in the Moonshot sleeve… still seems prudent”* for Intel). There is no strong guidance to alter the Moonshot approach in GPT-5.2’s analysis.

**Gemini’s analysis explicitly addresses the user’s hesitation with LEAPS and recommends simplifying**. It acknowledges the **complexity and risks of LEAPS** (theta decay, illiquidity, expiration timing) and suggests an **alternative: convert the Moonshot sleeve entirely to equities or leveraged ETFs**. Gemini’s stance is *“if you don’t understand LEAPS, do not trade them”*. Instead, it maps out a plan to **use common stocks for the Moonshot ideas**, retaining upside but avoiding options pitfalls. For example, the 3.5% that was to be in **Intel call spreads** can just go to Intel stock (now core). The 3.5% intended for **crypto-miner-turned-AI-host companies (CORZ, IREN, APLD)** can be held as equity in those names. And the 3% for **KTOS/AVAV LEAPS** can simply be KTOS/AVAV stock (or at most a 2x ETF for short spurts). It even lists specific 2× long ETFs (INTW for Intel, KTUP for Kratos) as *occasional tools* if leverage is desired. The core message: *simplify and de-risk the Moonshot sleeve by using straightforward instruments*.

**Bottom line:** GPT-5.2 didn’t propose changes to the Moonshot strategy (implicitly fine with the complex approach for those who can do it), whereas **Gemini advocates a more conservative, user-friendly approach** (or even deferring the sleeve entirely, given the user’s discomfort). This difference is directly responsive to the user’s note about not having started the Moonshot due to limited understanding of LEAPS – Gemini essentially says *“that’s fine, you can achieve similar asymmetric exposure without options or simply skip it”*. We will incorporate this advice in our recommendations, since it aligns with the user’s needs.

- **♢ SMCI vs. DELL – Removing Super Micro: GPT-5.2’s** analysis does not mention **Super Micro Computer (SMCI)** at all, which implies it fully endorsed the fund’s existing choice of **Dell (5%) as the play on AI servers** (since the original allocation had Dell, not SMCI). **Gemini’s** analysis, on the other hand, brings up SMCI specifically: it **recommends liquidating SMCI in favor of Dell**. This suggests that at some point the user or prior plan may have considered SMCI; Gemini emphatically advises against it. The rationale is that **the market shifted from “speed-to-market” (SMCI’s niche in 2023’s AI rush) to “scale-of-deployment” (Dell’s strength)**. In early AI hype, SMCI surged by quickly assembling GPU servers, but by 2025–26, large customers prefer the *reliability, supply chain and support* of Dell for scaling huge deployments. Additionally, Gemini notes **SMCI faced governance issues and margin pressures**, making it less attractive. Dell, conversely, has a *\$14.4B AI backlog and \$20B+ FY26 AI revenue guidance*, proving it’s winning the volume race.

Thus, **Gemini and GPT-5.2 likely differ only in that GPT-5.2 already assumed Dell (no SMCI), whereas Gemini explicitly ensures any SMCI exposure is removed.** The end conclusion is the same: **hold Dell (5%) as the premier AI server integrator** and **do not hold SMCI**. We mention this difference because the Gemini report made it a point, likely for completeness, whereas GPT-5.2 was silent (implying agreement by omission).

- **♢ Geopolitical & Macro Framing:** The analyses differ slightly in tone on macro factors:
- **Gemini’s report places a stronger emphasis on geopolitics** – it discusses how the **“Chip War” evolved into a “Power War”** and how U.S. industrial policy is favoring domestic tech infrastructure. It notes a *“distinct sovereign premium”* for U.S./ally-domiciled assets and explicitly cites **Intel and MP Materials** as beneficiaries of this trend (elevated to strategic asset status). It also highlights nuclear power’s re-rating in part due to energy security concerns.
- **GPT-5.2’s analysis, while aware of geopolitics, is more focused on supply/demand mechanics**. It certainly acknowledges **Taiwan risk** for TSMC and the CHIPS Act driving onshoring, but doesn’t use terms like “sovereign premium.” GPT-5.2’s framing is a bit more **bottom-up (industry trend)** whereas Gemini adds a **top-down strategic lens** (industry trends *plus* government actions and de-globalization).

In practical recommendations, this leads **Gemini** to double down on **Intel and MP (domestic capabilities)** and to maintain a close watch on regulatory risks (e.g. FERC potentially limiting data center co-location at power plants). **GPT-5.2** was already long MP and had Intel in options, but without explicitly crediting geopolitics as the reason (it was more about each company’s fundamentals). This isn’t a direct conflict – more of a difference in emphasis. Both agree on the end result (own Intel, own MP, watch Taiwan), but **Gemini’s articulation ties these moves to macro shifts in policy**, whereas GPT’s was rooted in microeconomic analysis.

- **♢ Allocation Nuances:** Finally, there are a few **minor allocation nuances** or stylistic differences:
- **CrowdStrike: GPT-5.2** floated increasing CRWD from 2% to ~3% over time[\[4\]](file://file_00000000532c71fdb514e9f4f39df014#:~:text=match%20at%20L1026%20Recommendation%3A%20Consider,CrowdStrike%20is%20arguably%20the%20%E2%80%9Cmust), whereas **Gemini** kept the target at 2% but labeled it a “Buy” (implying bullishness). In effect, both are positive on CRWD and suggest it could justify a bit more capital, but neither actually reallocated yet – it’s a soft difference (we will note CRWD as a potential to inch up).
- **Quanta (PWR): GPT-5.2** said hold **“at least 3%, with a bias to add on any weakness”**. **Gemini** similarly says 3% with a “Buy” stance. No real disagreement – both see 3% as a minimum and are enthusiastic about the thesis, meaning the fund could opportunistically increase PWR if conditions allow.
- **Defense (KTOS/AVAV): GPT-5.2** and **Gemini** both keep these small, but **Gemini** allocates *3% combined* (effectively 1.5% each) in its Moonshot section, a hair more than GPT’s 1% each core allocation. Gemini might be counting them as part of the Moonshot rework (since Intel moved to core, it gave a bit more room to defense). It’s a trivial difference in size, with both stressing these remain modest speculative bets.
- **No new sectors:** Neither analysis added new companies to the portfolio (with the exception of Intel’s status change). For instance, **no one suggested adding AMD, Marvell, Lam Research, etc.**, largely because the portfolio already covers the necessary bases. **GPT-5.2** explicitly reasoned against adding AMD for now, and against adding another fab tool maker like Lam (given overlap with AMAT). **Gemini** did not contradict this – it sticks to the same roster. So, any difference here is minimal; both are content with the current selection of stocks (apart from Intel’s promotion).

In summary, the **main actionable differences** are **Intel** (Gemini says add to core; GPT5.2 said keep as optional) and **Moonshot execution** (Gemini says simplify/defer; GPT5.2 didn’t address it). There’s also an explicit **“no SMCI”** message in Gemini, aligning with what GPT implicitly assumed. Other variations are subtle and generally reflect updated data or stylistic focus rather than fundamental disagreement. The next sections will use these insights to determine which recommendations to follow, especially in light of **current AI/datacenter trends** and whether the **fund’s allocation still looks optimal**.

## Alignment with Current AI/Datacenter Industry Trends (2025–26)

Both deep dives grounded their recommendations in the *real-world state of the AI industry* circa early 2026. Many of their points echo the latest market data and news. Here we highlight how key portfolio themes align with current developments:

- **🚀 Unprecedented AI CapEx Supercycle:** Both analyses note that **hyperscale tech firms massively ramped up capital expenditures on AI in 2025**, far beyond initial projections. In 2025 alone, the combined capex of Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and Meta **exceeded \$320 billion**, and 2026 is projected to top **\$500 billion**. This spending is building out what Gemini calls *“AI factories”* – giant data center campuses on a scale previously seen only in power infrastructure. Indeed, roughly **100 GW of new data center capacity** is expected globally by 2030 to support AI, a build-out valued at **\$3 trillion**. This **historic infrastructure surge directly benefits the Fund’s holdings**, which are effectively the suppliers and enablers for these AI megaprojects. It validates the strategy of investing in the **“picks and shovels”** of the AI boom: when \$100s of billions are spent on AI hardware, the profits accrue disproportionately to those providing **chips, fabs, power, cooling, and other physical inputs**.

- **🔗 Supply Bottlenecks and Pricing Power:** The core premise – that certain parts of the AI supply chain would become bottlenecks – is **playing out as predicted**, creating strong tailwinds for the portfolio:

- **Chip Fabrication & Packaging:** Demand for cutting-edge AI chips is vastly exceeding production capacity. TSMC’s leading-edge fabs (\<5 nm) are effectively **sold out years in advance** for AI orders. Forecasts show that by 2030, **~99% of all sub-5 nm output will be consumed by AI chips** (up from ~2.5% in 2023) – essentially AI will **cannibalize the entire advanced semiconductor industry**. This is evident already: Nvidia, AMD, Google, etc. are putting in huge orders that fully book TSMC’s 3 nm and future 2 nm pipelines. TSMC’s advanced packaging (CoWoS) capacity is also a constraint; even with aggressive expansion (doubling in 2024, targeting ~140k wafer-outs per month by end-2026), **demand still outstrips supply**. **Both deep dives cite this chronic shortage of leading-edge fabs and CoWoS lines** as a key reason TSMC can charge premium prices and should remain a major holding. Recent data supports this: TSMC’s gross margins hit ~62% in Q4’25, indicating pricing power, and it guided a record \$52–56B in 2026 capex (further evidence of overwhelming demand).

- **AI Accelerators (GPUs/ASICs):** Despite talk of AI chip supply shortages, Nvidia has managed to ship large volumes – but *only by prioritizing key customers and solutions*. By late 2025, the market shifted to selling entire **GPU supercomputer racks** (e.g. Nvidia’s HGX systems or the mentioned **GB200 “NVL72” rack with 72 GPUs + liquid cooling**) rather than individual cards. These racks cost \$2–3 million each and have months-long backlogs. **Nvidia’s backlog was ~3.6 million GPUs** at the start of 2026 – an enormous figure – and it has essentially monopolized **AI model *training*** even as some hyperscalers develop custom chips for *inference*. Both analyses note that **hyperscaler ASICs (like Google TPU, Amazon Trainium)** are on the rise **but have not dislodged Nvidia** in cutting-edge training; instead, they increase total demand (Jevons’ paradox). **Broadcom** is at the center of this ASIC trend as well, partnering on Google’s TPUs and recently signing a **\$11 billion deal with Google/Anthropic** to supply AI chips. The Gemini report flags Broadcom’s **\$73 billion AI-related backlog** and 100% YoY growth in AI chip revenue – concrete evidence that Broadcom’s custom silicon business is booming thanks to hyperscalers’ push to diversify from Nvidia. These industry facts align with the fund’s approach of **owning Nvidia *and* Broadcom**: Nvidia captures the huge “training compute” profits, Broadcom ensures we benefit from the *ASIC/efficiency side* of AI deployment. Both reports rightly anticipated this bifurcation and positioned the fund to win no matter which mix of GPU vs. ASIC prevails.

- **Memory (HBM): 2025–26 saw HBM memory become a critical choke-point.** Only three companies (SK Hynix, Samsung, Micron) make HBM, and demand for HBM on AI accelerators has far outpaced supply. Current industry data (cited in GPT-5.2) showed one supplier (Hynix) holding ~57% share, another (Samsung) stumbling to ~27%, and **Micron rising to ~16%** of this niche by stepping in with its HBM3E product. **Micron’s entire HBM production for both 2025 and 2026 is already pre-sold** – an extraordinary situation for a memory vendor. Micron also claims technical leadership (its HBM3E being ~30% more power-efficient than competitors’), which is crucial as data centers hit power limits. These trends confirm why the fund banked on Micron. In a traditional downturn, memory stocks suffer, but here **AI HBM demand is “cannibalizing” DRAM capacity and keeping prices high** despite weak consumer markets. Both analyses stress that memory is no longer a commodity but a **“strategic bottleneck”** tied to AI. This justifies **Micron’s 6% position – it’s directly in line with the** “physical bottleneck” theme (fuel for AI compute) **and stands to enjoy** pricing power and outsized earnings\*\* as long as HBM supply stays tight.

- **Networking (Data Center Switching):** The proliferation of AI clusters (with thousands of GPUs each) has pushed network infrastructure to its limits. **Ethernet has emerged as the preferred interconnect** (over InfiniBand) at massive scale, due to openness and cost, with efforts like 400 Gigabit and 800 Gigabit Ethernet to link AI nodes. **Arista Networks** – the portfolio’s pick – has been at the forefront of 400G/800G switching for cloud providers. Industry news shows **Arista’s revenue and orders surging** thanks to AI deployments (Arista reported record demand for its high-performance switches in 2025, often citing “AI spine” network build-outs). Both analyses reference this shift: GPT-5.2 notes *“Ethernet is now displacing InfiniBand as the preferred cluster interconnect, and Arista… is at the center of this transition”*. Gemini’s summary confirms **Arista 5%** weight and presumably draws on similar data (e.g. large orders from Meta or Microsoft for AI fabrics). The trend is clear: as AI data centers scale, **Arista’s technology has become essential**, which vindicates keeping Arista in the fund. In fact, GPT-5.2 mentioned that if evidence keeps mounting that **“all AI players standardize on Ethernet,”** one could argue for a higher Arista allocation – a nod to how strong this current is.

- **Datacenter Power & Cooling:** Perhaps the most striking 2025 development is how **electricity and cooling constraints emerged as top concerns** for AI scaling. Both analyses give extensive treatment to this:

  - **Power:** Training advanced AI models gobbles up *enormous* power. Estimates indicate a single large AI datacenter can require **100 MW or more** of power capacity. There’s no shame in leaving the Moonshot portion in cash or a broad index fund until you’re ready – better that than forcing trades you don’t fully understand.

- **If/when you want to utilize the Moonshot sleeve, use** simpler instruments (common stocks or ETFs) instead of LEAPS **to capture the themes originally intended.** We can largely mirror the Moonshot strategy with equities, as detailed in the Gemini deep dive:

- **“AI Landlords” (Data Center Infrastructure REIT-like plays):** The thesis here was that certain companies with large *power-provisioned datacenter footprints* (originally Bitcoin miners) can repurpose or rent out that capacity to AI cloud providers at huge profits. The three names identified were **Core Scientific (CORZ)**, **Iris Energy (IREN)**, and **Applied Digital (APLD)**. Instead of complex call spreads, one could simply **allocate, say, ~3–4% in equal parts to CORZ, IREN, APLD common stock**. These are very high-volatility stocks (they’ve moved ±50% in short spans), but as Gemini notes, *“the equity itself acts like a leveraged instrument”* due to beta \> 2.0. By holding the stock, you avoid theta decay and timing expiry issues – you can ride the trend if it plays out over a longer time. Recent developments: Core Scientific’s major hosting deal with CoreWeave remains in effect (the planned merger fell through, but that was arguably because CORZ shareholders expect more value); Iris Energy got a \$9.7B contract from Microsoft to provide cloud capacity (a huge validation of its pivot); and Applied Digital inked a 15-year, \$5B lease with a U.S. hyperscaler. They will be bumpy, but they directly benefit from AI demand for ready-to-go datacenter space.

- **Defense/Autonomy Leverage:** The Moonshot plan had additional exposure to **Kratos and AeroVironment via options**. We’ve already got 1% each in the core. If you wanted to amplify that, one simple way: allocate another ~1% from the Moonshot sleeve to each **(making KTOS ~2% and AVAV ~2%)** in common stock. That effectively doubles our bet on them, giving ~4% combined. This might be easier to stomach than buying long-dated calls. Alternatively, Gemini mentions a specific leveraged ETF: **Tuttle Capital’s 2x Daily Long KTOS (ticker KTUP)**. In theory, one could use a product like that to get a 2× exposure on Kratos for short periods (if expecting a catalyst like an contract announcement). However, be **cautious with leveraged ETFs** – as Gemini warns, they suffer from daily rebalancing and *volatility decay*, making them unsuitable for long holding periods. A simpler approach is just owning more of the stock or a mix of stock + some short-term call options around specific events. For most investors, sticking to stock is fine. **Summary:** If you want to allocate more to defense autonomy beyond the core 2%, do so with small additional equity positions in KTOS/AVAV or a very limited tactical use of a 2× ETF, rather than complicated LEAP spreads.

- **Crypto-related AI Plays (already covered above):** CORZ, IREN, APLD were the main ones. Another note: since these companies were originally crypto miners, their stocks may still correlate with crypto markets in the short term. - **Intel Leaps:** Originally the Moonshot sleeve included Intel call options (~3.5%). We have now effectively reallocated that into an actual 5% equity position in Intel in the core.
